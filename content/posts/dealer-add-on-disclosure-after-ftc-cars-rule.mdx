The FTC's CARS Rule (Combating Auto Retail Scams, 16 CFR Part 463) was supposed to take effect in 2024 and reshape how dealers disclose pricing and add-ons. It never did. If your compliance posture — or your vendor's marketing — still references "CARS Rule compliance," it is out of date in both directions: the rule is gone, and the obligations that remain are older and broader than the rule ever was.

Here is the current state of the law, with primary sources, and what it means for how you present add-ons.

## What happened to the CARS Rule

Two documents tell the whole story:

1. **January 27, 2025 — the Fifth Circuit vacated the rule.** In *National Automobile Dealers Association v. FTC*, No. 24-60013 ([opinion PDF](https://www.ca5.uscourts.gov/opinions/pub/24/24-60013-CV0.pdf)), the court set the rule aside on procedural grounds: the FTC skipped the advance notice of proposed rulemaking its own regulations required. The court did not rule that the conduct the rule targeted — hidden fees, worthless add-ons, bait pricing — was lawful. It ruled the FTC took a procedural shortcut.
2. **February 12, 2026 — the FTC withdrew the rule from the books.** A Federal Register notice ([docket FTC-2026-0133](https://www.regulations.gov/document/FTC-2026-0133-0001)) removed the CARS Rule from the Code of Federal Regulations to conform the rulebook to the court's decision.

The FTC did not appeal, and as of November 2026 it has not proposed a replacement. So: the CARS Rule is not current law, not pending law, and should not be cited as either.

## What still applies — and it is a lot

### 1. FTC Act Section 5: the law the CARS Rule was built on

Section 5 of the FTC Act (15 U.S.C. § 45) prohibits "unfair or deceptive acts or practices." It has covered auto retailing since long before the CARS Rule, and every add-on practice the rule singled out is still reachable under it:

- Advertising one price and charging another at the desk
- Charging for add-ons the customer never agreed to
- Presenting optional products as mandatory
- Charging for products that provide no benefit to the buyer

This is not theoretical. In 2026 — after the rule was vacated — the FTC sent [warning letters to 97 auto dealers and groups](https://www.crowell.com/en/insights/client-alerts/auto-dealers-the-ftc-is-back-in-the-drivers-seatwarning-letters-signal-renewed-federal-scrutiny) over pricing and add-on practices, under its existing Section 5 authority. The enforcement tool the agency lost was a *rule with per-violation penalties*; the underlying prohibition never moved.

### 2. The Used Car Rule: fully in force

The FTC's [Used Car Rule](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/dealers-guide-used-car-rule) (16 CFR Part 455) is a different regulation and was never part of the CARS litigation. It still requires a completed Buyers Guide on every used vehicle offered for sale, with the Spanish version mandatory when the sale is conducted in Spanish. Rule violations carry civil penalties of more than $50,000 per violation ($53,088 under the FTC's 2025 inflation adjustment; the figure adjusts annually). The FTC extended the rule's information-collection authority in 2026 — a signal it is staying.

If your lot discipline on Buyers Guides slipped while everyone watched the CARS litigation, that is the first thing to fix. (New to the three-label system? Start with [addendum vs. Monroney vs. Buyers Guide](/blog/addendum-vs-monroney-label-vs-buyers-guide).)

### 3. State law: where the specific add-on rules actually live

The most concrete disclosure requirements were never federal. Three examples — verify your own state with counsel:

- **California.** Vehicle Code [§ 11713.1(q)](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=VEH&sectionNum=11713.1.) prohibits a dealer from putting a supplemental price sticker on a new vehicle with an asking price above MSRP unless the sticker (a) says, in the largest print on the label other than the dealer's name, that the price is the dealer's asking price and not the MSRP, (b) shows the MSRP, and (c) lists each item not included in MSRP with its price — and labels any remaining difference "added mark-up."
- **Texas.** Motor vehicle advertising rules (43 Tex. Admin. Code § 215.250) treat items the dealer installed before sale — items the customer did not request — as part of the advertised price. Only charges like government fees and taxes may sit outside it. An "addendum item" that is really pre-installed cannot be presented as an optional extra on top of the advertised price.
- **New York.** New York City's [Used Car Consumer Bill of Rights](https://www.nyc.gov/assets/dca/downloads/pdf/businesses/Consumer-Bill-of-Rights-Used-Car-Poster-English.pdf) requires the itemized price of each add-on in writing and bars requiring add-ons as a condition of sale or financing. Statewide, the Attorney General has made add-on and fee disclosure an enforcement priority — a [September 2026 settlement](https://www.consumerfinancialserviceslawmonitor.com/2026/09/new-york-ag-settlement-signals-increased-scrutiny-of-dealer-fee-practices-and-add-on-products/) required dealerships to give buyers an itemized worksheet, including installed equipment, before the finance office.

Notice the pattern: every one of these points at the same artifact — **an itemized, truthful, vehicle-specific disclosure of what was added and what it costs.** That is a dealer addendum, done properly.

### 4. Manufacturer policies

Several manufacturers maintain their own policies on dealer-applied labels and over-MSRP pricing — some restrict using brand marks on supplemental stickers, others have warned dealers about advertised-price practices during allocation shortages. These are franchise matters rather than law, but they are enforceable through your dealer agreement. ## Replace "CARS compliant" with practices that hold up anywhere

Through 2024 a lot of software — ours included — was marketed against CARS Rule readiness. That framing is dead. The practices it pushed are not, because they were always just Section 5 and state law hygiene:

1. **Itemize every add-on with its own price** on the vehicle's label. No lumped "dealer package" lines.
2. **Keep required and optional visibly separate.** Pre-installed items roll into the asking price; optional products are [clearly marked as optional](/blog/required-vs-suggested-products-dealer-addendum).
3. **Show the math.** MSRP, additions, adjusted total — on the same label.
4. **Match the label to the listing.** The addendum on the windshield and the price on your website should tell the same story for the same VIN.
5. **Keep Buyers Guides current** on every used unit, in the right language.

To be direct about our own role: **DealerAddendums does not and cannot guarantee legal compliance.** Your processes and your state's laws decide that, and this article is not legal advice. What the platform does is make the good practice the default — every vehicle gets an itemized, current, consistently formatted addendum built from your actual product data, instead of whatever the last person typed. When disclosure is automatic, the gap between "our policy" and "what's on the glass" closes.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is the FTC CARS Rule in effect?**
No. Vacated January 27, 2025 (Fifth Circuit, No. 24-60013) and withdrawn from the CFR on February 12, 2026. No replacement has been proposed as of November 2026.

**Does that make add-on disclosure optional?**
No. FTC Act Section 5, state statutes like California Vehicle Code § 11713.1(q), and the Used Car Rule all still apply.

**Is the FTC still watching dealers?**
Yes — 97 warning letters went to dealers and groups in 2026 under Section 5.

**Does addendum software make a dealer compliant?**
No software guarantees compliance. It makes itemized, accurate disclosure the default, which is the practice every surviving rule points toward.
