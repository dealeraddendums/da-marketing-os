Dealers shopping for "window sticker software" land in a market that actually contains two different categories wearing similar names. One is **lot-merchandising software**: tools that produce attractive spec labels, photos, QR codes, and sometimes Buyers Guides, to present inventory well. The other is **addendum software**: tools whose core job is the dealer's own pricing disclosure — what was added to the car, what it costs, and what the adjusted price is.

They overlap at the window. They diverge in purpose, data, and risk. This is a comparison of the categories — not of named vendors — with a checklist at the end.

## What each category is for

**Window-sticker / lot-merchandising software** answers: *"How do we present this vehicle well?"* Typical outputs:

- Spec-style window labels for used cars (which have no factory Monroney): equipment, packages, options decoded from the VIN
- Photo-ready presentation: value bullets, CARFAX-style badges, QR codes to the listing
- Buyers Guide printing as an included form
- Sometimes pricing, usually as a single advertised price

Its center of gravity is the **used-car department and the merchandising team.** The data it leans on is VIN-decoded factory equipment.

**Addendum software** answers: *"What did we add to this vehicle, what does it cost, and is that disclosed the same way everywhere?"* Typical outputs:

- The addendum label itself: MSRP, each dealer-added product with its price, adjusted total, required disclosure language
- A product library with rules (make, model, trim, year, body style, mileage, fuel type) so items apply to the right vehicles automatically
- Required vs. suggested product handling — in-the-price items separated from [clearly optional offers](/blog/required-vs-suggested-products-dealer-addendum)
- FTC Buyers Guides and info sheets from the same vehicle records
- A website tie-in so the VDP shows the same disclosure as the windshield

Its center of gravity is **pricing integrity across the whole store** — new and used — and the data it leans on is *your* product data joined to a live [inventory feed](/blog/how-addendum-software-works-inventory-feed-dms).

## The real difference: presentation data vs. pricing data

A VIN decode can tell any tool that a truck has a 5.0L V8 and a tow package. It cannot tell any tool that *your store* installs a $595 bedliner on every new truck and offers a $1,299 protection package on used units under 60,000 miles. That second kind of data — your products, your prices, your rules — is the entire substance of an addendum, and managing it is what addendum software is built around.

That difference shows up in failure modes:

- A merchandising label with a stale option list is **embarrassing**.
- An addendum with a wrong price or an optional product presented as required is **a dispute at the desk and a disclosure problem** — the kind regulators pursue under the FTC Act and state law ([what still applies after the CARS Rule](/blog/dealer-add-on-disclosure-after-ftc-cars-rule)).

Which is why the categories engineer differently: merchandising tools invest in templates and photos; addendum platforms invest in rules engines, required/suggested separation, price-change propagation, and print history.

## When each makes sense

**Lot-merchandising software fits when:**

- Your priority is used-car presentation — spec labels, photo overlays, value bullets
- Your add-on program is minimal (few or no priced dealer products)
- The merchandising team owns the output, and pricing disclosure lives elsewhere

**Addendum software fits when:**

- You install or charge for dealer products on new or used units and need each one itemized at its correct price, per vehicle
- You price above or below MSRP and need the math shown on the glass
- You want the website VDP and the windshield to carry the same disclosure
- You have multiple rooftops and need consistent templates and product rules across stores
- You print FTC Buyers Guides and want them coming from the same inventory data instead of a separate form pad

**Running both is legitimate.** A store with a heavy used-car merchandising program may keep its merchandising tool for presentation and run addendum software for pricing disclosure, Buyers Guides, and the new-car side — the documents simply do different jobs. What to avoid is two tools printing *conflicting prices* for the same VIN; whichever system owns pricing disclosure should be the only one printing priced labels.

## Decision checklist

Work through these in order. Answers on the left point to merchandising software; answers on the right point to addendum software.

1. **What's the primary document?** Spec/presentation label → merchandising. Priced disclosure of dealer add-ons → addendum.
2. **Do you charge for dealer-installed products?** No → merchandising may be enough. Yes → addendum software, because each product must be itemized at the right price per vehicle.
3. **Where do prices come from?** VIN decode and a single advertised price → merchandising. Your own product library with per-vehicle rules → addendum.
4. **Do new cars need labels too?** Used-only → either. New and used → addendum (new cars already have a Monroney; what they need is the dealer's supplement beside it).
5. **Must the website match the windshield?** Nice-to-have → either. Required → addendum software with a VDP integration.
6. **Who answers for it?** Marketing manager → merchandising. Desk, GSM, and compliance → addendum.
7. **Multi-rooftop?** Single store → either. Group needing shared templates, group pricing rules, and per-store control → addendum platforms are built for it.
8. **Buyers Guides?** Occasional, hand-filled → either. Every used unit, English and Spanish, from live inventory → addendum.

If three or more answers land on the right-hand side, you are shopping for addendum software, whatever the tool calls itself.

## Frequently asked questions

**Aren't addendums and window stickers the same thing?**
No — a window sticker is usually a merchandising spec label; an addendum is the dealer's priced disclosure of added products. (Full breakdown: [addendum vs. Monroney vs. Buyers Guide](/blog/addendum-vs-monroney-label-vs-buyers-guide).)

**Can one tool do both?**
Partially, from both directions. Decide which document is primary for your store and pick the category built around it.

**Do I need both?**
Some used-car-heavy stores run both. Make sure only one system prints priced labels.

**Which carries compliance weight?**
The addendum and the Buyers Guide — those are the documents disclosure laws care about.
