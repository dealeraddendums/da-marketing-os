## Why Inventory Sync Matters for Pricing Transparency

Car buyers expect to see one number: the price they will actually pay. When a sticker on the lot does not match the number on the website or the number quoted at the desk, buyers lose trust fast. That mismatch is also becoming a legal problem, not just a reputation problem.

DealerAddendums now connects directly to Tekion's Inventory API, a system that lets outside software read live vehicle data from a dealer's Tekion DMS (dealer management system — the software that tracks inventory, deals, and pricing). This connection means every addendum your dealership prints reflects the same price your Tekion system shows right now, not the price from this morning's data pull.

This matters more than ever because California has a new law aimed squarely at pricing accuracy.

## The California CARS Act: What Dealers Need to Know

The California CARS Act is Senate Bill 766, Chapter 354, Statutes of 2025. Governor Newsom signed it on October 6, 2025. It becomes operative October 1, 2026. It applies to licensed California dealers selling light-duty vehicles, meaning vehicles under 10,000 pounds gross vehicle weight rating.

The law requires dealers to show an all-in price — the full price a buyer will pay before government fees and optional add-ons, with no hidden markups baked in. This is often called all-in pricing dealership compliance. The goal is simple: the price advertised, the price posted, and the price at the desk should match.

Here is where dealers get confused, so let's be precise.

### California CARS Act vs. FTC CARS Rule: Two Different Laws

The federal government tried something similar first. The FTC CARS Rule (16 CFR Part 463) was a federal regulation from the Federal Trade Commission. It would have required similar pricing disclosures nationwide. But the Fifth Circuit Court of Appeals vacated that rule in January 2025, and the FTC formally withdrew it in 2026. The federal CARS Rule is not current law. It is not pending. It is not enforceable anywhere.

The California CARS Act is a separate, state-level law. California lawmakers modeled it on the vacated federal rule, borrowing its structure and some of its language. But it stands on its own as California state law, independent of anything happening at the federal level.

If a vendor, a blog post, or a sales rep tells you the "CARS Rule" applies to your dealership nationwide, ask which one they mean. If they cannot answer, they may not understand the difference. For California dealers, the state Act is what matters, and it takes effect October 1, 2026.

## What an Addendum Actually Is (And Why It Has to Be Accurate)

An addendum is a printed sticker or sheet, usually displayed next to the federal Monroney label (window sticker), that lists dealer-added items and their prices. Examples include paint protection, VIN etching, nitrogen-filled tires, or market adjustment pricing. Addendums are not new, but under pricing transparency laws like California's, they need to match whatever price the dealer actually charges at the point of sale.

Other documents work the same way. The FTC Buyers Guide is a federally required used-car disclosure form (separate from the now-withdrawn CARS Rule) that tells buyers whether a vehicle is sold "as is" or with a warranty. A CPO Info Sheet documents what a certified pre-owned inspection covered. All three documents — addendums, Buyers Guides, and CPO sheets — need the same thing: accurate, current data pulled from the same source dealers already use to manage inventory.
