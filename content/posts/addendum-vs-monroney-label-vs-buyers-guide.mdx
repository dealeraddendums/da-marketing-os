Walk a franchise dealer's lot and most vehicles carry more than one window label. Shoppers usually call all of them "the sticker." They are not the same thing. One is required by federal law on new cars. One is required by federal law on used cars. And one is the dealer's own label — the addendum — which is where dealer-added products and prices are disclosed.

Mixing them up causes real problems: compliance violations, pricing disputes at the desk, and OTD ("out-the-door" price) negotiations that start with distrust. Here is what each label is, who requires it, and what belongs on it.

## The Monroney label: the federal new-car sticker

The **Monroney label** is the factory window sticker on every new car. It is named for Senator Almer "Mike" Monroney, who sponsored the Automobile Information Disclosure Act of 1958. That law — 15 U.S.C. § 1232 — requires the manufacturer, not the dealer, to affix a label to every new automobile before it ships.

The Monroney must show, among other things:

- The **MSRP** (Manufacturer's Suggested Retail Price — the manufacturer's recommended selling price for the vehicle as built)
- Standard equipment, plus factory-installed options and their prices
- The destination charge (what the manufacturer charges to deliver the car)
- The EPA fuel-economy label
- NHTSA crash-test star ratings
- Domestic and foreign parts content

Two rules matter most for dealers:

1. **Only the manufacturer creates it.** A dealer cannot print, edit, or reissue a Monroney.
2. **It stays on the car.** Removing or altering the label before the vehicle is delivered to the buyer is a federal violation, with a fine for each offense. That is why an addendum is always placed *beside* the Monroney — never over it.

## The Buyers Guide: the federal used-car sticker

The **Buyers Guide** is required by the FTC's [Used Car Rule](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/dealers-guide-used-car-rule) (16 CFR Part 455), in force since 1985. Any dealer who sells more than five used vehicles in a 12-month period must display a Buyers Guide on every used vehicle offered for sale.

The Buyers Guide discloses warranty terms. The three common configurations are:

- **As-Is — No Dealer Warranty.** The dealer makes no warranty. Some states do not allow as-is sales.
- **Implied Warranties Only.** Used in states that prohibit as-is sales.
- **Dealer Warranty.** The dealer warrants the vehicle, and the guide must state what percentage of repair costs the dealer pays and which systems are covered.

Three details dealers get wrong:

1. **The Spanish version is mandatory when the sale is conducted in Spanish.** Not optional, not a courtesy.
2. **It applies to demos and program cars** being sold as used — anything with a title history.
3. **The form itself is prescribed.** The FTC revised the form in 2017; using the pre-revision layout is a violation.

The penalty scale is serious. Rule violations can draw civil penalties of **more than $50,000 per violation** ($53,088 under the FTC's 2025 inflation adjustment; the figure adjusts annually). The FTC has run multi-agency compliance sweeps checking exactly this — whether a guide is displayed, filled out, and in the right language.

The Used Car Rule remains fully in force in 2026. Do not confuse it with the CARS Rule, which was vacated — we cover [what dealers still have to disclose after the CARS Rule](/blog/dealer-add-on-disclosure-after-ftc-cars-rule) separately.

## The addendum: the dealer's own label

An **addendum** (also called a supplemental sticker or addendum label) is the label the *dealer* creates. It lists products and services the dealer added to the vehicle after it left the factory — window tint, protection packages, nitrogen-filled tires, accessories, regional fees — each with its price, and shows the adjusted asking price.

Notice three things about it:

1. **Every added item is listed with its own price** — $895 and $195 in this example, not a lumped "dealer package."
2. **The total is shown** so the MSRP-plus-additions math is visible: $21,375 MSRP plus $1,090 in options equals $22,465.
3. **The bottom carries a disclosure** stating the addendum was added by the dealer, not the manufacturer, and is not an authorized factory sticker.

No federal statute says "every dealer must print an addendum." But the moment a dealer charges more than MSRP or adds priced products, disclosure obligations attach from two directions:

- **State law.** California Vehicle Code [section 11713.1(q)](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=VEH&sectionNum=11713.1.) is the clearest example: a new-vehicle supplemental sticker with an asking price above MSRP must say it is the dealer's asking price, must show the MSRP, must list each added item with its price, and must label any remaining difference "added mark-up."
- **Federal law.** Section 5 of the FTC Act prohibits unfair or deceptive practices. Charging for add-ons that were never disclosed, or misrepresenting them as required, is the kind of conduct the FTC pursues under Section 5 — rule or no rule.

So the practical answer to "is an addendum required?" is: *if you add products and charge for them, an itemized addendum is how you stay on the right side of the laws that do apply.*

## Side by side

| | Monroney label | Buyers Guide | Dealer addendum |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Applies to** | New vehicles | Used vehicles | Any vehicle with dealer add-ons |
| **Required by** | Federal law (1958 Act) | Federal law (FTC Used Car Rule) | No federal mandate; state disclosure laws + FTC Act apply to its content |
| **Created by** | Manufacturer | Dealer (prescribed FTC form) | Dealer (own format) |
| **Shows** | MSRP, equipment, fuel economy, safety | Warranty terms | Dealer-added products, prices, adjusted total |
| **Can the dealer change it?** | No | Must use the FTC form | Yes — it is the dealer's document |

## How the three work together on one vehicle

On a new car: the Monroney stays where the factory put it, and the addendum goes on an adjacent window with the dealer's added items. The shopper can compare the two and see exactly what the dealer added and what it costs. On a used car: the Buyers Guide is displayed as the FTC requires, and an addendum (or an info sheet) rides alongside with reconditioning, certification, or accessory charges.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is a dealer addendum required by law?**
No federal law requires one. But if you charge for added products, state disclosure laws (like California's supplemental-sticker statute) and the FTC Act's ban on deceptive pricing require those charges to be truthful and clearly disclosed. An itemized addendum is the standard way to do that.

**Can a dealer change or remove the Monroney label?**
No. Federal law requires it to remain on the vehicle until delivery to the buyer. The addendum is placed beside it, never over it.

**Does a new car need a Buyers Guide?**
No — the Used Car Rule covers used vehicles. A titled demo or program car sold as used does need one.

**Where does the addendum go?**
Next to the Monroney on new cars, next to the Buyers Guide on used cars, and never covering either.
